Cells sense the mechanical properties of their surrounding extracellular matrix (ECM), including elasticity, viscoelasticity, and plasticity, and this mechanosensing regulates cell fate during development and disease. While cells are typically assumed to sense ECM mechanics through direct adhesive interactions, many biomedically important cells such as mesenchymal stem cells, chondrocytes, and cancer cells rapidly secrete their own “nascent” matrix that occupies the cell-ECM interface, raising questions as to how these cells can sense the mechanical properties of the ECM. As a postdoc in the [Chaudhuri lab](https://chaudhurilab.stanford.edu/) at Stanford, I investigated this problem and studied how chondrocytes sense and respond to hydrogel scaffolds with tunable elastic, viscoelastic, and plastic properties.

In my study [1], I showed that hydrogel rheological properties mediate the accumulation of the nascent matrix at the cell-hydrogel interface. I showed that viscoelastic and plastic hydrogels facilitate the penetration of the nascent matrix into the surrounding network, while elastic hydrogels confine the nascent matrix in the pericellular space, thus imposing solid stress on the cells. I showed that solid stress drives an osteoarthritis- and injury-like response characterized by NF-kB-driven inflammation, co-activation of anabolism and catabolism, and a striking chondroprogenitor-like migratory behavior within the hydrogel. I examined these confined migration events in detail, and showed that chondrocytes leverage mitosis as a propulsive mechanism to breach their confining matrix and escape solid stress. These findings show that matrix-secreting cells sense the solid stress arising from ECM confinement – which is regulated by the rheological properties of the ECM – and that solid stress provides a mechanobiological framework for understanding cartilage osteoarthritis and injury.
